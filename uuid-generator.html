How UUID v4 / GUID Generation, 122-Bit Entropy & Database Keys Work

Why Distributed Systems Use UUIDs Instead of Auto-Incrementing Integers

A Universally Unique Identifier (UUID)—also known as a GUID (Globally Unique Identifier) in Microsoft ecosystems—is a 128-bit identifier formatted as 32 hexadecimal digits split into five hyphen-delimited groups (8-4-4-4-12). Unlike traditional auto-incrementing SQL integers (1, 2, 3...), which expose total record counts in public URLs and collide when merging records across microservices or offline mobile apps, UUID Version 4 lets any client or server generate globally unique primary keys independently without coordinating with a central database sequence. Our generator uses the browser's hardware-backed Web Crypto API (crypto.getRandomValues) rather than predictable pseudo-random math functions.

UUID v4 Entropy Bits & Collision Probability Formula + Worked Example

Out of 128 total bits in a Version 4 UUID, 4 bits specify version 4 and 2 bits specify the RFC variant (8, 9, a, or b), leaving 122 cryptographically random bits:

Total Possible UUID v4 Keys = 2122 ≈ 5.3 × 1036  |  Collision Risk ≈ n² / (2 × 2122)

Worked Example: Inspecting the UUID structure xxxxxxxx-xxxx-4xxx-yxxx-xxxxxxxxxxxx:

The 13th hex digit is always fixed to 4 (identifying Version 4 random), and the 17th hex digit y is always 8, 9, a, or b.

Even if your application generates 1 billion UUIDs every second for 85 years (2.68 × 1018 IDs), the probability of a single duplicate collision remains below 50%.
