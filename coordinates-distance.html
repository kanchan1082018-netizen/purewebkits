Complete User Guide to GPS Coordinates Distance, the Haversine Formula, Bearing & Midpoint

Why Great-Circle (Haversine) Distance Is Used Instead of Flat-Grid Geometry

Because the Earth is curved rather than a flat plane, applying ordinary 2D Euclidean distance to latitude and longitude degrees produces inaccurate results—especially at higher latitudes where lines of longitude converge toward the poles. To measure the shortest surface path between two GPS points, navigation systems, aviation planners, logistics tools, and GIS applications use the Haversine formula, which computes the great-circle distance along a spherical Earth model of mean radius R = 6,371 km (3,958.761 miles or 3,440.065 nautical miles). Alongside distance, calculating the initial forward azimuth (bearing) and spherical geographic midpoint helps visualize route direction and halfway coordinates. Haversine Great-Circle Distance Formula

Converting decimal degrees to radians (φ = lat × π/180, λ = lon × π/180) with Δφ = φ₂ − φ₁ and Δλ = λ₂ − λ₁:

a = sin²(Δφ / 2) + cos(φ₁) × cos(φ₂) × sin²(Δλ / 2)
c = 2 × atan2(√a, √(1 − a))  |  Distance d = R × c  (R = 6,371 km)

Helpful Tips When Working With GPS Coordinates

  • Use Signed Decimal Degrees: For example, New York City (40.7128° N, 74.0060° W) is entered as Latitude 40.7128 and Longitude -74.0060.
  • Remember Straight-Line Distance vs. Driving Distance: Great-circle distance measures the direct "as-the-crow-flies" arc over the Earth's surface, which is shorter than road driving routes.
  • Watch Opposite Direction Bearings: Reversing Point A and Point B keeps the distance identical while shifting the initial bearing by roughly 180° on short routes (and along the reverse great-circle arc on long routes).

Worked Example: Between New Delhi (28.6139°, 77.2090°) and Mumbai (19.0760°, 72.8777°), the Haversine distance is approximately 1,148.03 km (713.35 mi / 619.89 nmi) on an initial heading of 203.2° (SSW).
