Complete User Guide to Prime Numbers, Composite Numbers, and Trial Division

Why Prime Numbers Are Foundational to Mathematics and Computing

A prime number is a natural number greater than 1 that has no positive divisors other than 1 and itself—such as 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, and 97. Numbers greater than 1 that have at least one additional divisor are called composite numbers (such as 4, 6, 9, or 49), while integers less than or equal to 1 (such as 0, 1, and negative integers) are neither prime nor composite. Prime numbers serve as the multiplicative building blocks of integers via unique prime factorization and underpin modern public-key cryptography, hashing, and algorithm design. Square-Root Trial Division Rule

If a number n > 1 is composite and has a factor a > 1, then n = a × b, meaning at least one of a or b cannot exceed √n:

Check divisors d from 2 up to ⌊√n⌋ (skipping even numbers after 2)
If n mod d === 0 for any d ≤ ⌊√n⌋ → Composite; otherwise → Prime

Common Prime Number Pitfalls to Avoid

  • 1 Is Not a Prime Number: By mathematical convention, 1 is excluded from primes so that every integer greater than 1 has a unique prime factorization.
  • 2 Is the Only Even Prime: Every other even number is divisible by 2 and therefore composite.
  • Odd Numbers Like 49 or 91 Can Still Be Composite: Always check up to ⌊√n⌋—for example, 49 = 7 × 7 and 91 = 7 × 13.

Worked Example: Testing 97 gives trial-division bound ⌊√97⌋ = 9. Since none of 2, 3, 5, 7 divides 97 evenly, 97 is a prime number with divisors 1 and 97.
